Compare PDFly to Other PDF Tools

How PDFly stacks up against Adobe Acrobat, Smallpdf, iLovePDF, and PDF24 on cost, privacy, and features.

Choosing a PDF tool usually comes down to a handful of practical questions: does it cost anything, does it require an account, does the file get uploaded to a server or stay on your device, and does it cap how much you can do in a day. This page compares PDFly against four of the most widely used PDF tools on the web — Adobe Acrobat, Smallpdf, iLovePDF, and PDF24 — on exactly those points, without exaggerating what any of them do or guessing at pricing details that change over time.

PDFly's own position is straightforward: every tool, including the five AI-powered ones (chat with a document, summarize it, translate it, get a plain-language explanation, and generate a quiz from it), is free with no account and no daily limit, and most tools process your file locally in your browser rather than uploading it to a remote server. That combination — free, account-free, and largely local — is the main thing that sets PDFly apart from most of the field, and it's why this page exists: so you can see exactly where that trade-off helps you and where a more established, feature-rich competitor might actually serve you better.

None of the four tools compared here are worse products in any absolute sense — each was built with a different audience and business model in mind, and each is very good at what it targets. Adobe Acrobat remains the deeper choice for teams that need to collect signatures from multiple external parties, run advanced print-production edits, manage document workflows at scale, or administer a paid suite across an entire organization with centralized licensing and permissions. If your job involves negotiating redlines on a fifty-page contract or routing a document through six approvers, Acrobat's dedicated tooling for that is hard to match with a free browser tool.

Smallpdf and iLovePDF are both strong, well-established freemium suites with large catalogs of conversion and editing tools, slick interfaces, and enough brand recognition that many people default to them without comparing alternatives. The trade-off with both is a daily cap on the free tier — use enough tools in a day and you'll be prompted to upgrade to a paid plan for unlimited or batch use. For someone converting one file a week, that limit rarely matters. For someone processing a stack of documents in one sitting, it can turn into friction that a tool with no daily cap avoids entirely.

PDF24 is closest to PDFly in spirit: it's free, and its web-based tools don't require an account either. Where it diverges is delivery — PDF24 is best known for its downloadable desktop application, which is primarily built for Windows and needs to be installed before you get its full feature set. PDFly instead stays entirely in the browser on any device, which matters if you're on a Mac, Chromebook, phone, or a work computer where you can't install new software.

Each comparison below is organized the same way: a short summary of what the competitor does well, a side-by-side table covering cost model, account requirements, and where files get processed, plus two or three feature rows specific to that competitor — e-signature request workflows for Acrobat, daily usage limits for Smallpdf and iLovePDF, platform and installation requirements for PDF24. Where a competitor's specific pricing tier, plan name, or feature limit could reasonably change between now and when you're reading this, we've described the general model (subscription, freemium, or free) instead of quoting a figure that might already be out of date. If a specific number matters to your decision, we'd encourage checking that competitor's own pricing page directly before you commit to a plan.

None of this is meant to talk you out of using Adobe Acrobat, Smallpdf, iLovePDF, or PDF24 — all four are legitimate, widely used tools with loyal users for good reason. The goal here is simply to lay out where PDFly's free, account-free, browser-based approach fits your specific task, and where a more established competitor's dedicated features are worth the extra cost, account, or install.
