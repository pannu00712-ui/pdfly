The 50MB Email Attachment That Made Me Question Everything

I was sitting in my car in the parking lot of a FedEx office, which should have been my first clue that something had gone terribly wrong. I had just spent three hours putting together a presentation for a prospective client. High-res images, embedded charts, the works. It looked beautiful. It also weighed in at 47MB, and Gmail had politely informed me that I was about 22MB over their attachment limit.

I could have just used a file-sharing service. I knew that. But there was something about sending a client a link instead of an actual file that always felt a little off to me. Like I was making them do extra work. So instead, I drove to FedEx, plugged in a USB drive, and printed the whole thing, thinking I would just mail it. Yes, I actually did that. I physically printed a 47MB presentation and mailed it in an envelope. In 2023. This is not a joke.

That moment in the parking lot, holding a stack of paper that had taken three hours to print, I realized that I had a serious problem with PDF files. Not just that one presentation, but all of them. My computer was full of huge PDFs that I could barely send anywhere. My clients were waiting on documents that I could not deliver. And I was, apparently, resorting to mailing paper like it was 1997.

It was humiliating. And it was entirely unnecessary.

What I didn't understand about file size

At that time, I didn't really understand why some PDFs were huge and others were tiny. I thought it was just the number of pages. A hundred-page document is heavier than a ten-page document, right? That makes sense. But then I would get a twenty-page PDF that was 2MB, and a six-page PDF that was 40MB, and I could not figure out why.

Eventually, I learned what was actually happening. Those six pages had high-resolution images embedded in them. Every photo, every logo, every chart was being saved at full resolution, which is completely unnecessary for a document that people are going to read on a screen. Nobody needs a 300 DPI company logo in a PDF that's just going to be emailed around. That's like serving a five-course meal when all they wanted was a sandwich. It's too much.

I also learned that the way a PDF is saved matters. If you save it with standard compression, it's going to be much heavier than if you save it with good compression. And some tools are just better at compressing than others. I spent an embarrassing amount of time learning this by trial and error, which involved a lot of trial, a lot of error, and a lot of swearing.

The time I compressed a file so badly it was unreadable

Let me tell you about a specific moment where I tried to compress a PDF and absolutely butchered it. I had a contract I needed to email to a client in another state. It was a legal document, so it was important that it looked professional. I found a random online compression tool, uploaded the file, and hit compress. When I opened the result, the text was blurry. Not just a little blurry — like someone had smudged it with a wet thumb. The logos looked like pixelated squares. I would have been embarrassed to send that to anyone, let alone a client I was trying to impress.

I had to go back, find the original, and start over. And of course, I had already closed the browser tab with that random compression tool, and I could not remember which one I had used. So I had no idea which tool had ruined my document. That was a fun afternoon.

That experience taught me that not all compression is good compression. There's a difference between making a file smaller and making it unusable. A good compression tool preserves readability. It might slightly reduce image quality, but text remains crisp, logos remain clean, and the document still looks professional. A bad compression tool just shreds everything down to the lowest possible quality and calls it a day. And that experience cost me a few hours and some serious frustration.

What actually matters when you compress a PDF

After that disaster, I started paying attention to what the compression was actually doing. The first thing I look at now is whether the text remains sharp. Text is the most important part of most PDFs — if it's blurry, the whole document is useless. Some compression tools treat text and images the same way, which is a mistake. Text should be compressed differently, with priority given to readability.

The second thing I look at is images. I've learned that you don't need to keep every image at print resolution. For a document that's going to be viewed on a screen, you can lower the resolution quite a bit before anyone notices. But you can't lower it too much, or it will look bad. The sweet spot is somewhere around 150 DPI for most business documents.

And the third thing I look at is whether the tool actually works consistently. Some compression tools work great on one file and then completely fail on another. I've seen tools that can't handle scanned documents, or they choke on large files, or they add watermarks. That inconsistency is what drove me crazy.

One more thing I learned — sometimes the file size isn't actually a problem. A lot of people worry about file size because of email attachments, but if you're using a document sharing platform or a cloud service, you can send big files that way. But sometimes you don't have that option, especially if you're dealing with someone who is not tech-savvy. In those situations, compression becomes essential.

What I still hate about compressing PDFs

If I'm being honest, the thing I still hate the most is that some tools make you wait. You upload your file, and then there's a loading bar. And you're just sitting there, watching it move slowly, wondering if it's going to work or if you're going to get an error at 98% and have to start over. That has happened to me enough times that I have trust issues with progress bars now.

I also still get frustrated when a tool compresses the file but doesn't tell me how much it reduced it. I want to know what I got — was it 10% smaller or 80% smaller? That helps me decide if I need to compress it more or if I can just send it as-is. A lot of tools just give you the file back and expect you to be okay with it, which I'm not.

And yes, I still have that lingering fear from the time I ruined a contract. Every time I compress a PDF, I open it immediately to check the quality. I can't help it. That experience left a permanent mark on me. I will never trust a compression tool completely without checking the result first.

What I do now instead of stressing about it

These days, I just use the same tool for compression that I use for everything else. It took me a while to stop jumping between different tools and just settle on something that works consistently. I realized that every time I tried a new tool, I was spending more time learning how to use it than I was actually getting work done. And I still had that nagging worry about whether the quality would be good enough.

Now I use PDFly's Compress PDF tool when I need to shrink a file. The reason is honestly pretty boring — it just works. The text stays sharp, the images are readable, and it actually tells me how much space I saved. It doesn't make me wait forever, and it doesn't add watermarks. Most importantly, it doesn't make me relive that day in the FedEx parking lot with a stack of paper and a broken spirit.

I still keep a backup of every PDF before I compress it though. I don't think I'll ever stop doing that. Some habits are too hard to break, even when you find a tool you trust.
