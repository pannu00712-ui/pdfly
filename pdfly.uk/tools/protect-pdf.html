How to password protect a PDF

  1. Drop your PDF into the tool above, or click to browse your device.
  2. Enter a password and confirm it. Choose your permissions — allow printing, copying, or modifying.
  3. Click "Protect PDF" and download your encrypted document. Everything runs in your browser — nothing is uploaded.

How PDFly protects your PDF

PDFly uses the pdf-lib library to apply strong encryption to your PDF. Here's how it works:

  • Standard PDF encryption: Your PDF is encrypted using the PDF Standard Security Handler (RC4, 40-bit), the same encryption format built into the PDF specification since version 1.1 and supported by virtually every PDF reader, including older ones.
  • User password: The password you set is required to open the document. Without it, the file is unreadable.
  • Owner password: An additional owner password is generated automatically to manage permissions like printing, copying, and editing.
  • Permission controls: You can restrict printing (low or high quality), copying text, and modifying the document.

The encryption happens entirely in your browser — your password and file data never leave your device.

Why protect a PDF with a password?

  • Business contracts and reports: Ensure only authorized parties can view sensitive business information.
  • Financial statements and tax documents: Protect personal and financial data from unauthorized access.
  • Legal documents and certificates: Add an extra layer of security to official paperwork.
  • Medical records: Comply with privacy requirements by protecting health information.
  • Email attachments: Send sensitive PDFs securely by sharing the password separately.
  • Cloud storage: Even if your cloud account is compromised, encrypted PDFs remain protected.

Understanding permissions

  • Allow printing: When checked, users can print the document. If unchecked, most PDF readers will disable the print function.
  • Allow copying text: When unchecked, text selection and copying are disabled in compatible PDF readers.
  • Allow modifying: When unchecked, most PDF readers restrict editing, adding annotations, or filling forms.

Important: Permission restrictions depend on the PDF reader being used. Some readers may not enforce all permissions, so treat them as a helpful deterrent rather than absolute protection.

Choosing a strong password

  • Length matters: Use at least 12 characters. Longer passwords are harder to crack.
  • Mix character types: Combine uppercase and lowercase letters, numbers, and symbols (e.g., MyS3cur3P@ssw0rd!).
  • Avoid common words: Don't use "password," "123456," or easily guessable phrases.
  • Use a password manager: Consider using a password manager to generate and store strong, unique passwords.

Before you protect your PDF

  • Save your password: Store it in a password manager or write it down and keep it secure. There's no recovery option.
  • Test the protected file: After downloading, try opening it with the password to confirm it works correctly.
  • Keep the original: The original file remains unchanged. Keep a copy in case you need an unprotected version.
  • Share the password separately: If emailing the protected PDF, send the password through a different channel (e.g., text message or phone call).

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the password — there's no way to recover it.
  • Using a weak or commonly used password that's easy to guess.
  • Sharing the password in the same email as the protected PDF.
  • Deleting the original file before testing the protected version.
  • Protecting a PDF that's already encrypted without unlocking it first.

Troubleshooting

  • The protected PDF won't open: Double-check that you're entering the correct password. Passwords are case-sensitive.
  • Permissions aren't being enforced: Some PDF readers (especially older ones) may not respect permission settings. Try opening in a modern reader like Adobe Acrobat or Chrome.
  • I forgot my password: PDFly cannot recover lost passwords. If you need the content, you'll need to find the original unprotected file.
  • The file is very large after protection: Encryption adds minimal overhead — any size increase is usually negligible.
